# Gaze tracking: what a webcam can measure on a chessboard, and what to build

Research sweep, 2026-11-12. Scope: browser eye tracking for Kiebitz, to be built **after the course**. This note covers the libraries, what their accuracy means on a chessboard, the chess eye-tracking literature, which gaze metrics survive webcam noise, how to collect gaze during real games and training sessions, and privacy. Candidate analyses have their own notes:

- [gaze-threat-check.md](gaze-threat-check.md): how much of a think goes to the opponent's side and king zone, and whether that drops before missed-threat mistakes.
- [gaze-last-move-reaction.md](gaze-last-move-reaction.md): whether and how fast the player looks at the opponent's last move.
- [gaze-tunnel-vision.md](gaze-tunnel-vision.md): staying stuck in one board region (an Einstellung signal) before mistakes where the better move was elsewhere.
- [gaze-relevance-latency.md](gaze-relevance-latency.md): in puzzle and "guess the move" sessions, how fast the player's gaze reaches the region that matters.

Numbers come from the linked sources. Where a number comes from my own simulation or arithmetic, I say so. Speculation is marked.

## Summary

1. **Webcam gaze is much less accurate than the vision doc assumes.** The vision says "roughly one square (~100 px)". Realistic mean error in unsupervised home use is **about 2° to 7° of visual angle**. On a typical desktop board one square is about 2.5° (95 px); on a laptop it is about 1.9°. So the error is **1 to 3 squares**, not one. Peer-reviewed lab numbers are better (Labvanced: 1.4° against an EyeLink 1000), but that engine is proprietary and tied to the Labvanced platform.
2. **Square-level analysis is impossible; half-board and quadrant analysis works.** In my simulation, with 4° mean error on a desktop board, only 15% of gaze samples land in the correct square. 76% land in the correct quadrant and 87% in the correct half. 3x3-square zones (king zone, last-move zone) work only when averaged over many moves.
3. **WebEyeTrack (MIT) is the library to use.** WebGazer is GPL-3.0 and no longer guaranteed to be maintained (since 24 February 2026). WebEyeTrack's real gain is less drift over a 20-minute session (+20% error instead of +49%), not a smaller starting error. It is a small academic project (npm `webeyetrack` 0.0.2, 67 GitHub stars), so we should expect to maintain a fork.
4. **Every move is a free calibration point.** When a player clicks or drags a piece, they are almost certainly looking at it. Using the from- and to-squares of the player's own moves to correct drift is the single most important engineering idea in this note (it is how WebGazer self-calibrates from clicks). It turns a 30-minute rapid game into about 40 to 80 recalibration events.
5. **The literature supports region-level metrics.** The strongest chess eye-tracking findings are at the level of board regions, not squares: experts look at the relevant half of the board earlier ([Sheridan & Reingold 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00941/full)), and experts gradually look away from a familiar but wrong corner region, while novices do not ([Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)). Fixation-level findings (fixations between pieces, fixation counts, visual span) need lab trackers.
6. **No study links gaze to errors inside one player's real games.** All published work compares experts with novices on puzzles in a lab. Linking a player's gaze habits to their own lost points would be new research, so every Kiebitz gaze finding is a hypothesis until we validate it, starting with the owner's own data.
7. **Real games with gaze are only possible on Lichess, at rapid or slower.** The Lichess Board API allows rapid, classical and correspondence; blitz only for direct challenges, games against the AI and bulk pairings. Bullet is impossible. Chess.com has no play API, and its Fair Play Policy lists "browser extensions" among the tools not to use during play. For chess.com players and for fast time controls, gaze has to come from **training sessions** inside Kiebitz (positions from their own games, or Lichess CC0 puzzles).
8. **Sample sizes are the bottleneck.** A 2380 player makes few missed-threat mistakes per rapid game. Linking gaze to such errors needs on the order of 30 to 50 error events, which means dozens of rapid games with the webcam on (my estimate). Training sessions produce data about 5 to 10 times faster.
9. **Privacy: keep video and raw gaze in the browser.** Under GDPR, gaze data becomes special-category biometric data when it is used to identify someone; the UK ICO lists gaze-based recognition as a behavioural biometric. Gaze can also reveal health information. Kiebitz should process frames locally, upload only a few aggregated numbers per move, ask for explicit opt-in, and never infer emotions (that would fall under the EU AI Act's definition of an emotion recognition system).

### Real-world problems, with evidence

- **Drift.** Error grows over a session: WebGazer +49% and WebEyeTrack +20% over about 20 minutes ([WebEyeTrack paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19544v1)). Labvanced runs a 6.7-second "Gaze Control" check every three minutes, or before every trial in its strictest tier, because drift builds after calibration ([lab note](https://www.labvanced.com/content/research/en/lab-notes/2026-10-webcam-eyetracking-2-0/)). A crowdsourced study found shorter sessions predict better data quality ([Lau & Kasneci 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02898)). A rapid game lasts 20 to 60 minutes, so recalibration during play is required (see section 5).
- **Lighting.** In Labvanced's data, participants whose face stayed within 5% of the calibration brightness had a median error of 5.49% of the screen diagonal. Those whose face got more than 10% darker had 10.94%, twice the error ([lab note](https://www.labvanced.com/content/research/en/lab-notes/2026-10-webcam-eyetracking-2-0/)). Evening play with a changing room light is a real risk, so Kiebitz should track face brightness and flag segments.
- **Glasses.** Many validation studies simply exclude glasses wearers: Van der Cruyssen et al. required participants "who did not wear glasses during the study" ([BRM 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-023-02221-2)). I found no good number for the accuracy cost of glasses. **Open question**, and it matters because many adult chess players wear them.
- **Head movement.** Head movement shifts the estimate; Labvanced's head-pose correction cut median error from 6.37% to 5.72% of the diagonal ([lab note](https://www.labvanced.com/content/research/en/lab-notes/2026-10-webcam-eyetracking-2-0/)). EMC-Gaze's error halves when the head is still (2.92° vs 5.79°, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12388)).
- **Drop-outs.** In online studies, many people fail calibration: 49 of 125 MTurk participants passed WebGazer calibration and validation in one study ([Yang & Krajbich, JDM](https://cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1930297500008512/type/journal_article)); 53 of 118 were excluded in a Max Planck online study of deep-learning gaze models ([Saxena et al.](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3528824_1/component/file_3528825/mus-23-sax-01-deep.pdf)). Kiebitz users are motivated and can retry, but we should expect a share of users for whom gaze never works.
- **Effect sizes shrink.** Three classic gaze effects replicated online with WebGazer, but 20 to 27% smaller ([Van der Cruyssen et al. 2023](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-validation-of-online-webcam-based-eye-tracking-the-replicatio/)).
- **Sampling rate.** Webcam trackers run at most at the camera or screen rate, about 30 to 60 Hz, and jsPsych's WebGazer extension defaults to one sample every 34 ms ([Gorilla docs](https://support.gorilla.sc/support/tools/task-builder-2/eye-tracking), [jsPsych docs](https://www.syncog.ppls.ed.ac.uk/sort/general/jspsych/docs/extensions/jspsych-ext-webgazer.md)). Saccades last 30 to 40 ms, so individual fixations and saccades cannot be resolved reliably ([thesis citing Holmqvist](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/40709/Eindwerkstuk_Bachelor_KI_Mark_Wessel_6008283_final.pdf?sequence=1)). **Use dwell time in regions, not fixation counts or durations.**
- **Performance.** BlazeGaze itself is tiny (0.88 ms per inference on a desktop CPU, outside the browser, [paper Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19544v1)). The cost is the MediaPipe face mesh per frame. WebEyeTrack runs in a Web Worker, and its August 2026 commits reduce main-thread cost and remove a GPU stall in calibration. During play there is no engine running (fair play), so the CPU budget is fine. Inference cost on low-end laptops is **not measured yet**.

## 2. What the accuracy means on a chessboard

My own arithmetic (assumptions: typical board sizes at default zoom; 1° at distance *d* = *d*·tan 1°):

| Setup | One square | One square in degrees | 1° in device pixels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24" 1080p desktop, board 760 px, 60 cm | 26 mm (95 px) | **2.5°** | 38 px |
| 27" 1440p desktop, board 960 px, 65 cm | 28 mm (120 px) | **2.5°** | 49 px |
| 15.6" 1080p laptop at 125% scaling, board 600 CSS px, 50 cm | 17 mm | **1.9°** | 49 px |
| 13.3" 1080p laptop at 150% scaling, board 520 CSS px, 45 cm | 15 mm | **1.9°** | 51 px |

For comparison, lab studies use larger boards: one square was 3.4° in Sheridan & Reingold's EyeLink studies ([2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)), and their tracker was accurate to well under 1°. **Kiebitz should show a large board in gaze mode** (as big as the screen height allows) and ask laptop users to plug in a monitor if they can.

**Simulation** (my own; Python, isotropic Gaussian error, gaze spread uniformly over the board; script in the session scratchpad, not in the repo). Share of gaze samples that land in the correct region:

Desktop, square = 2.5°:

| Mean error | Square (8x8) | 2x2 blocks (4x4 grid) | 3x3 grid | Quadrants (2x2) | Own vs opponent half |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5° | 0.45 | 0.74 | 0.82 | 0.91 | 0.95 |
| 2° | 0.34 | 0.66 | 0.76 | 0.88 | 0.94 |
| 3° | 0.21 | 0.52 | 0.66 | 0.82 | 0.90 |
| 4° | 0.15 | 0.41 | 0.56 | 0.76 | 0.87 |
| 5° | 0.11 | 0.34 | 0.49 | 0.71 | 0.84 |

Laptop (square = 1.9°) at 4°: square 0.11, quadrants 0.70, halves 0.84. Adding 2° of uncorrected drift costs about 2 to 3 points for quadrants and halves.

For a **3x3-square zone** (king zone, last-move zone) on a desktop at 4° error: a sample is counted in the zone 56% of the time when the player really looks at its centre, 9% of the time when they look 3 squares away and 2% at 4 squares away. Averaged over a 15-second think at 30 Hz, a true 30% dwell share and a true 10% dwell share are observed as about 0.15 and 0.09, and still separate well for single moves (AUC 0.87 to 0.96 with per-move drift of 2° to 3° SD). Caveat: this assumes independent noise per sample; real webcam noise is partly correlated, so treat these as upper bounds.

**Conclusions for design:**

- **Do not use squares or pieces as units.** Use the two halves, the four quadrants, a 3x3 grid, and a few chess-defined zones of about 3x3 squares (each king zone, the opponent's last-move zone).
- **Measure shares and latencies averaged over many moves**, never single fixations. Observed shares are pulled towards the base rate, so compare a player only with themselves (error moves vs good moves, this month vs last month), with the same setup.
- **Report the per-session validation error** (from move clicks, see section 5) next to every gaze finding, and drop sessions above a threshold (for example 5°).

## 3. Chess eye-tracking literature: what differs by expertise

| Study | Players | Task, tracker | Finding | Webcam-measurable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Reingold, Charness, Pomplun & Stampe 2001](https://www.cs.umb.edu/~marc/pubs/reingold_et_al_psysci2001.pdf) | Check detection: 10 experts (CFC 1950 to 2352, mean 2117), 10 intermediates, 20 novices | Minimised 3x3 board, "is the king in check?" | Experts were faster (861 ms vs 1,087 vs 1,207 ms) and more accurate (1.3% errors vs 2.6% vs 3.7%), made fewer fixations, and placed proportionally more fixations **between pieces** rather than on them. 15.9% of expert trials had no eye movement at all (novices 1.6%). Flicker task: much larger visual span for structured positions | No (needs sub-square precision and fixation detection) |
| Charness, Reingold et al. 2001 (described in [Sheridan & Reingold 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00941/full)) | Experts (mean Elo 2238) vs intermediates (1786) | Choose-a-move | Experts put a greater proportion of fixations on pieces relevant to the best move, visible already in the **first five fixations** | Partly, if "relevant" is coarsened to a region |
| Reingold & Charness 2005 (same source) | Experts vs intermediates | First 10 s of choose-a-move | Experts finish a perceptual encoding phase (short fixations) quickly, then move to problem solving (longer fixations) | No (fixation durations) |
| Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet 2008, *Cognition* (described in [Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)) | Experts | Problem with a familiar mate-in-5 and a better mate-in-3 | Experts said they were looking for a better solution, but their eyes kept returning to the squares of the familiar one (Einstellung effect). A related 2008 study found the familiar solution lowered experts to the level of players about three standard deviations weaker | Yes, if the competing ideas are in different board regions |
| [Sheridan & Reingold 2013, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/) | 17 experts (CFC mean 2223, range 1876 to 2580), 17 novices | Einstellung move in a corner region; EyeLink 1000, square 3.4° | Experts' looking time in the Einstellung region **declined linearly over the trial** (F(1,66) = 13.12); novices' did not. Time to first fixation on the region: 407 ms vs 719 ms. Experts who found the optimal move disengaged most strongly | **Yes** (quadrant-sized region, time course over the think) |
| [Sheridan & Reingold 2014, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00941/full) | 17 experts (CFC mean 2223), 24 novices | Two versions of each problem; swapping one bishop for a knight swaps which **half of the board** is relevant | Both groups dwelled longer on the relevant half in total, but only experts did so in their **first dwell** (2.19 vs 1.39 fixations, relevant vs irrelevant). Experts found the best move in 93% of trials, novices 52%. Within experts, reaction time correlated with Elo at r = -0.617 | **Yes** (half-board regions, early-trial window) |
| [de Winter et al. 2023, Journal of Expertise](https://www.journalofexpertise.org/articles/volume6_issue1/JoE_6_1_deWinter_etal.pdf) | Experts FIDE 2001 to 2464 (mean 2251), intermediates ~1500, novices <1200 | Gaze-contingent reconstruction; find-the-best-move puzzles | Experts lose much of their advantage when peripheral vision is blocked. In puzzles, experts made far fewer fixations (21.5 per puzzle in the main analysis) and had a lower SD of fixation location. The SDs in the tables are roughly 110 to 210 px across groups and conditions, so group differences are tens of pixels | Not usable: the group differences are tens of pixels, smaller than webcam error (150+ px). The peripheral-vision result is a key **caveat**: not fixating a region does not mean not seeing it |
| [Küchelmann et al. 2024, Frontiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11540784) | 58 players: 17 experts (Elo/DWZ ≥ 1850), 20 intermediates, 21 novices | 11 Lichess mate puzzles on a real DGT board, mobile eye-tracking glasses (0.5°) | Experts solved more (medium puzzles: 98.5% vs 56.3% vs 28.6%), with far fewer fixations (easy: 36 vs 132 vs 257). Experts put more fixations on **empty squares** (medium: 71.7% vs 57.2% vs 58.0%). No link between gaze and success at trial level | Number of fixations: roughly via think time. Empty-square share: no |
| [Le Louëdec et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.08155) | 30 players, 9 experts (Elo 1759 to 2150) | Predict gaze saliency from board images | Small data; gaze maps had to be averaged per task; best correlation with human gaze maps 0.66 | Shows how scarce chess gaze data is |

**What this means for Kiebitz:**

- The robust, webcam-sized findings are **when** strong players attend to the right region (early in the think) and **how they leave** a tempting wrong region. Both are about time courses over halves or quadrants.
- Kiebitz's users (1800 to 2400) are the "experts" in most of these studies. Expert-vs-novice differences tell us which signals carry information, not what a 2380 player does wrong. **Use within-player comparisons**: the same person's error moves against their good moves.
- **Nobody has linked gaze to move errors in real games**, or within one player. The closest is gaze-based expertise classification (CEEGE project, [Guntz et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04486), with preliminary gaze results). This is both the opportunity (G10 in [prior-work-gap-map.md](prior-work-gap-map.md)) and the main risk.

## 4. Ranked gaze metrics

Ranked by (likely to survive webcam noise) × (plausibly linked to lost points) × (actionable). All are computed per think (from the opponent's move to the player's move) in board-relative coordinates, with the board oriented to the player.

| Rank | Metric | Exact definition | Webcam survival | Evidence | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Opponent-side share** (threat-check ratio) | Dwell time with gaze on ranks 5 to 8 (from the player's side) ÷ dwell time on the board, per think. Variant: share in the opponent's piece-occupied quadrants | High (halves: 84 to 95% correct) | Indirect: relevance and threat detection literature | [gaze-threat-check.md](gaze-threat-check.md) |
| 2 | **Last-move check** | (a) latency from the opponent's move to first gaze sample in the 3x3 zone around the opponent's to-square (plus from-square, for discovered attacks); (b) dwell share in that zone in the first 3 s | Medium (3x3 zone, aggregated) | Experts fixate relevant regions faster ([2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/), [2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00941/full)) | [gaze-last-move-reaction.md](gaze-last-move-reaction.md) |
| 3 | **Region persistence (tunnel vision)** | Quadrant with the most dwell in the first third of the think; its dwell share in the last third; slope of its share over think quartiles; number of quadrant switches per 10 s | Medium-high (quadrants: 70 to 82% correct) | Einstellung studies ([Bilalić 2008 via Sheridan 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)) | [gaze-tunnel-vision.md](gaze-tunnel-vision.md) |
| 4 | **Relevant-region latency** (training mode only) | Time from position shown to first ≥ 300 ms dwell in the quadrant or half that holds the solution's key squares; relevant-region share in the first 5 s | High (halves/quadrants; relevance known from the solution) | Directly replicates [Sheridan & Reingold 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00941/full) | [gaze-relevance-latency.md](gaze-relevance-latency.md) |
| 5 | **Regions visited before the move** | Number of distinct quadrants (or 3x3-grid cells) with ≥ 300 ms of dwell before the move; Shannon entropy of dwell over the 9 cells of a 3x3 grid | Medium (noise inflates visits, so the dwell threshold is needed and must be fixed per setup) | Expert scan patterns are more concentrated ([de Winter 2023](https://www.journalofexpertise.org/articles/volume6_issue1/JoE_6_1_deWinter_etal.pdf)), but that is the opposite direction from "check more regions". Ambiguous | Feature inside notes 1 and 3, not a finding on its own |
| 6 | **King-zone attention** | Dwell share in the 3x3 zone around each king, per think, split by whether the opponent has attacking chances (e.g. engine eval swing or attackers near the king) | Medium (3x3 zone) | Indirect | Variant inside [gaze-threat-check.md](gaze-threat-check.md) |
| 7 | **Pre-move sweep** (blunder check) | In the last 2 s before the move, share of gaze outside the moved piece's quadrant | Low (about 60 samples, and the eyes are on the piece being moved) | None direct | Later, as a feature |
| 8 | **Off-screen time** | Share of a think with no face, closed eyes or gaze outside the screen; count of off-screen episodes > 2 s | High | None in chess | Answers "distraction", not "how you see the board". Privacy-sensitive, feels like surveillance. Later, opt-in |
| 9 | Gaze dispersion | SD of gaze position in px | Fails: group differences of tens of pixels vs webcam error of 150+ px, and confounded by think length | [de Winter 2023](https://www.journalofexpertise.org/articles/volume6_issue1/JoE_6_1_deWinter_etal.pdf) | Drop |
| 10 | Fixation count, fixation duration, fixations between pieces / on empty squares, visual span | Fixation-level | Fails (30 Hz, square-level precision needed) | Strong in the lab ([Reingold 2001](https://www.cs.umb.edu/~marc/pubs/reingold_et_al_psysci2001.pdf), [Küchelmann 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11540784)) | Drop |
| 11 | Pupil size (cognitive load), blink rate (fatigue) | | Pupil: fails with webcams. Blink rate: WebEyeTrack reports eye state, but I have no validation source for blink rate as a fatigue measure at webcam frame rates (speculation) | | Drop for now |

## 5. Engineering: making the numbers usable

- **Move clicks as calibration.** Each of the player's own moves gives two screen points the player almost certainly looked at: the piece picked up and the target square, in the few hundred ms around the click. WebGazer already treats clicks as calibration data ([WebGazer site](https://webgazer.cs.brown.edu/)). Kiebitz can (a) measure per-session error as the distance between predicted gaze and the clicked square centre, and (b) refit the affine correction or WebEyeTrack's few-shot head every N moves. This gives a validation number for every session at no cost to the user. **Untested assumption**: that strong players, especially with premoves or quick recaptures, look at the target square at click time. We need to check this on the owner's data first.
- **Explicit calibration**: 9-point calibration at the start, plus a 5-second check between games. Never during a game, so play is not interrupted; move clicks cover drift inside the game.
- **Quality flags per segment**: face brightness change > 10% from calibration, head moved > N cm (WebEyeTrack gives `faceOrigin3D`), face lost. Segments with flags are excluded from analysis.
- **Everything in the browser.** Gaze capture, calibration, region assignment and per-move feature extraction run in the UI (Web Worker). The insights service receives only per-move features (a dozen numbers per move) and does the cross-game statistics. No new service, no queue, no extra Azure resource.

## 6. Collecting gaze: real games vs training sessions

| Setting | Time controls | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real Lichess games in the Kiebitz board via the Board API** | Rapid, classical, correspondence; blitz only for direct challenges, games vs the Lichess AI and bulk pairings ([Lichess API spec, Board tag](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lichess-org/api/master/doc/specs/lichess-api.yaml), [eBoards page](https://lichess.org/page/eboards)). Rapid means estimated duration (base seconds + 40 × increment) of 480 to 1499 s ([Lichess FAQ](https://lichess.org/faq#time-controls)), e.g. 10+0, 15+10. Seeks accept 0 to 180 minutes and 0 to 180 s increment ([seek spec](https://github.com/lichess-org/api/blob/master/doc/specs/tags/board/api-board-seek.yaml)) | Real stakes, real errors. Exact square coordinates (our own board). Exact move times from the game stream | Only Lichess; no bullet; blitz only against friends or the AI. Fair Play: a real-time Board API game allows no engine, explorer, book, tablebase or advice ([Lichess fair play](https://lichess.org/page/fair-play)), so Kiebitz must show nothing analytic during play. Needs an OAuth token with `board:play` scope, which can live only in the browser (PKCE), so no secret on our side |
| **Chess.com real games** | | | Not possible. No play API, and the Fair Play Policy says "Do not use chess engines, software of any kind, bots, plugins, browser extensions or any tools that analyze positions during play" ([chess.com Fair Play Policy](https://www.chess.com/legal/fair-play)). A gaze extension does not analyse positions, but the wording is broad and a moderator warned that extensions interacting with the board "could be detected as something disallowed" ([forum](https://www.chess.com/clubs/forum/view/chrome-extension-approval-for-fair-play?lc=1)). Not worth risking a user's account. **Drop** |
| **Kiebitz training sessions** ("guess the move" from the player's own critical positions, or Lichess puzzles) | Any. A per-position timer can imitate bullet (3 to 5 s), blitz (10 to 20 s) or rapid (60+ s) thinking | Works for chess.com users. Relevant regions are known in advance (engine best move, the threat). 5 to 10 times more decisions per hour than a rapid game. Lichess puzzles are CC0 ("Use them for research, commercial purpose, publication, anything you like"), 6.19 million rated and themed puzzles ([database.lichess.org](https://database.lichess.org/)) | Lower stakes than real games. Puzzles signal "there is something here", which changes how people search. "Guess the move" positions from the player's own games avoid this |

**Recommendation:** start with training sessions (works for every user and every time control, data comes fast, and relevance is known), then add Lichess Board API rapid games for the users who want real-game gaze.

## 7. Privacy and GDPR

- **Is gaze biometric data?** Under GDPR Art. 4(14), biometric data is personal data from "specific technical processing" of physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics "which allow or confirm the unique identification" of a person ([Art. 4 text](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/4/adopted/data.html)). The UK ICO lists "gaze-based recognition (eye tracking)" among behavioural biometric techniques, and treats biometric data as special-category when it is used to uniquely identify someone, or when it reveals another special category such as health "regardless of whether you intend to infer this" ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data)). Kiebitz does not use gaze to identify anyone, but gaze traces can reveal identity, age, emotional state and health conditions ([Kröger, Lutz & Müller 2020](https://dl.ifip.org/hal-03378980v1)). **Treat raw gaze as sensitive regardless of the legal classification.**
- **Webcam frames** are personal data (face images). They must never leave the browser.
- **EU AI Act.** The AI Act's definition of biometric data (Art. 3(34)) drops the "unique identification" condition, and an emotion recognition system is one that infers "emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data" (Art. 3(39), [AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-3)). Recital 18 excludes physical states such as fatigue ([recital 18](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/recital-18)). Kiebitz should measure **where** attention goes, never infer tilt, frustration or "intentions" from the face or gaze. Using facial blendshapes (which WebEyeTrack outputs) for mood would move us into emotion recognition.
- **Design rules:**
  1. Explicit, separate opt-in for gaze, revocable at any time; the camera is off by default.
  2. Frames and raw gaze samples stay in the browser memory and are discarded after feature extraction. Only per-move aggregates (region shares, latencies, quality flags) are uploaded, linked to the game and move number.
  3. A user can delete all gaze features with one action (fits the CRUD requirement pattern).
  4. Do a DPIA (GDPR Art. 35) before launch; eye tracking is a new technology that processes data about behaviour.
  5. No gaze data in research exports without separate consent.

## 8. Fit with the constraints

All capture and feature extraction run in the browser. Storage is a `gaze_move_features` table in the insights database. Lichess Board API games are imported into the games service like any other game afterwards. No third container, no queue, no extra Azure service. **Not MVP**: the course decided gaze is built after 2026-11-24.
